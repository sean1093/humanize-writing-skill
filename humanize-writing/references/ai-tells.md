# AI Tells: Catalog

This catalog lists patterns that make English prose read as machine-written. Any one of them alone proves nothing, since humans use all of these. The tell is in the **density** and the **co-occurrence**: a piece that delves into a rich tapestry, showcases three pivotal points, and ends with "Ultimately, …" is unmistakable.

## 1. Vocabulary

Models favor these words far beyond their frequency in human writing.

| Overused | Usually better |
|---|---|
| delve (into) | look at, dig into, get into |
| tapestry, mosaic, symphony | (cut the metaphor) |
| testament (to) | shows, proves |
| landscape, realm, sphere, arena | field, world, or name the actual thing |
| navigate (the complexities of) | deal with, handle, get through |
| leverage, harness, utilize | use |
| foster, cultivate, nurture | build, encourage, grow |
| empower, enable, unlock, unleash | let, help, allow |
| elevate, enhance, amplify | improve, raise, or be specific |
| showcase, highlight, underscore | show, point out |
| crucial, pivotal, vital, paramount, essential | important, or show why it matters |
| robust, seamless, comprehensive, holistic | (say what makes it good) |
| intricate, nuanced, multifaceted, complex | complicated, or name the parts |
| vibrant, bustling, rich, dynamic | (describe the actual thing) |

## 2. Stock phrases

- **Openers**: "In today's fast-paced world", "In the ever-evolving landscape of", "When it comes to", "In an era where", "Have you ever wondered", "Picture this:", "Imagine a world where"
- **Signposting**: "It's important to note that", "It's worth noting/mentioning", "Let's dive in", "Let's break it down", "Here's the thing:", "Buckle up", "Without further ado"
- **Framing**: "plays a crucial/pivotal role in", "serves as a", "stands as a testament to", "is a testament to", "at its core", "at the end of the day", "the key lies in"
- **Closers**: "In conclusion", "In summary", "To sum up", "Ultimately,", "All in all", "By doing X, you can Y", "The future of X is bright", "only time will tell", "the possibilities are endless"
- **Audience hooks**: "Whether you're a seasoned pro or just starting out", "Whether you're X or Y, …"

## 3. Sentence-level patterns

- **Negation-then-reframe**: "It's not just X, it's Y." / "This isn't about X. It's about Y." / "not only X but also Y". This is the single most recognizable modern tell. Use it at most once per piece, and only when the contrast is real.
- **Rule of three**: "fast, reliable, and scalable"; three parallel clauses; three bullet points. Models default to triplets. Use two, or four, or one.
- **Trailing participle commentary**: "…, highlighting the importance of collaboration." / "…, ensuring a seamless experience." / "…, making it a must-read." An "-ing" tail that editorializes on the sentence before it.
- **Colon or em-dash reveal**: "The secret? Consistency." / "There's one thing that matters — trust." Fine once. Rhythmic overuse is a tell.
- **Rhetorical question then immediate answer**: "So what does this mean for you? It means…"
- **Elegant variation**: cycling synonyms to avoid repeating a noun ("the city… the metropolis… the urban center"). Humans just repeat the word.
- **Hedge stacking**: "may potentially", "can often help to", "it could be argued that".
- **Vague authority**: "Experts agree", "Studies show", "Many believe" with no source.
- **Uniform length**: sentence after sentence in the 15–25 word band, each one grammatically complete and balanced.

## 4. Paragraph and document structure

- Intro that restates the prompt or topic before saying anything.
- Every paragraph built the same way: topic sentence, two support sentences, summary sentence.
- Every paragraph roughly the same length.
- Mechanical transitions starting paragraphs: "Firstly / Secondly / Finally", "Moreover", "On the other hand".
- A conclusion that recaps everything just said.
- Symmetric coverage: every option gets equal pros and cons, whether or not they deserve it.

## 5. Tone and stance

- Relentlessly positive, encouraging, or "inspiring".
- No opinions, or opinions immediately softened by balance ("However, it's important to consider…").
- Generic examples ("a small business owner", "a busy professional") where a real writer would use a specific one.
- Over-explaining things the reader already knows.
- Moralizing or safety caveats that the context didn't call for.

## 6. Formatting and punctuation

- Em dashes (—) everywhere, especially in spaced form " — ".
- Bold lead-in bullets ("**Scalability:** The system…").
- Headers on short pieces. Title Case Headers.
- Emoji as bullet markers or section decorations.
- Curly quotes and the ellipsis character "…" mixed into otherwise plain-typed text.
- Perfectly consistent formatting with no human slips. (Don't add fake typos, though. That's a cheap trick and it backfires.)

## 7. Chat residue

Strip anything that only makes sense in a chatbot conversation:
- "Certainly!", "Great question!", "Absolutely!", "I hope this helps!", "Feel free to…", "Let me know if…"
- "As an AI…", knowledge-cutoff disclaimers
- "Here's a revised version:" left at the top of the text
