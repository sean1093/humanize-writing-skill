# Genre Playbooks

The core rules (specific over generic, varied rhythm, no stock phrases, no invented facts) apply everywhere. What changes between genres is the register, the format, and which tells matter most. Read the section for the genre you're working on.

## Contents
1. Resume / CV
2. Reports (work, research, school)
3. Speaking practice (scripts, answers, self-introductions)
4. Short genres (email, LinkedIn, blog, marketing copy)

## 1. Resume / CV

Recruiters read hundreds of these, and AI-generated resumes have a very recognizable sound. Bullets are expected here, so keep the format. What gives a resume away is the content of the bullets.

**Tells specific to resumes**
- Inflated verbs: spearheaded, orchestrated, championed, revolutionized, pioneered, leveraged, harnessed, synergized
- Self-description adjectives: results-driven, detail-oriented, passionate, dynamic, highly motivated, proven track record, team player
- Every bullet with identical structure: "Verb + noun + resulting in X% improvement in Y"
- Round, suspiciously neat metrics everywhere ("increased efficiency by 30%") with no context
- A summary paragraph made entirely of adjectives

**What to do**
- Use plain, accurate verbs: built, wrote, ran, fixed, cut, moved, set up, led (only if they actually led).
- Put the specific thing first: what system, what customer, what scale. "Rewrote the billing export (Python, ~2M rows/night); job went from 3 h to 20 min."
- Vary bullet shape. Some bullets have a number and some don't. Some are one line, some two.
- **Every metric must come from the user.** If you don't have one, write `[metric?]` or leave the bullet unquantified. A made-up number on a resume is a lie the candidate will be asked about in an interview.
- Keep the summary to 1–2 sentences, or cut it. Say what they do and for whom, not what kind of person they are.
- In bullets, drop the first person and the articles naturally ("Built…", not "I built a…"), but don't strip them so hard that it reads like telegraph text.

**Before / after**
> - Spearheaded cross-functional initiatives to streamline onboarding processes, resulting in a 40% increase in efficiency.
> - Built the new-hire checklist in Notion and got IT, HR and the team leads to actually use it; laptop setup went from [X] days to [Y].

## 2. Reports (work, research, school)

Reports are allowed to be formal. A formal report still reads as human when it makes clear claims supported by specific evidence, in a logical order. AI reports read as smooth, balanced, and empty.

**Tells specific to reports**
- An introduction that restates the assignment or title
- "This report will explore/delve into…" roadmap paragraphs
- Section after section of the same length, each ending with a mini-summary
- "Moreover / Furthermore / Additionally" chaining paragraphs together
- Conclusions that recap instead of conclude ("In conclusion, X, Y, and Z are all important factors…")
- Hedging that is vague ("may potentially impact") instead of precise ("in 3 of the 5 sites we checked")
- Vague citations ("studies show") with no source

**What to do**
- Lead with the finding. Business reports should open with the bottom line; academic ones should state the thesis or argument in the first paragraph.
- Use headings if the report is long enough to need navigation. Headings should be informative ("Churn is concentrated in month 2"), not generic ("Analysis").
- Let section lengths follow the content.
- Use "we/I" if the field allows it (most do now), and active voice when the actor matters.
- Hedge precisely. State scope, sample, and uncertainty in concrete terms.
- Cite real sources the user gave you. Never invent references, DOIs, or quotes. Use `[citation needed: …]` placeholders instead.
- End with what follows from the findings (a recommendation, implication, or open question), not a summary.
- Mild sentence-length variation still matters in formal writing. Don't let every sentence come out at 20–25 words.

## 3. Speaking practice (scripts, answers, self-introductions)

Here the target is **natural spoken English**, which differs from good written English. Text written to be read aloud that sounds like an essay is the tell. Listeners notice it right away, and so do interviewers and IELTS/TOEFL examiners.

**How speech differs from writing**
- Shorter sentences, and clauses added on with "and", "but", "so", "because"
- Contractions almost always (I'm, it's, didn't, I'd)
- Discourse markers that buy time and signal structure: "So,", "Actually,", "I mean,", "The thing is,", "To be honest,", "Anyway,"
- Restating and self-correction: "It was hard. Well, not hard exactly, more like… tedious."
- Concrete, personal, present-tense storytelling ("So I'm standing there, and my manager walks in…")
- Fewer nouns made from verbs ("we decided", not "the decision was made")
- Simpler vocabulary than writing. "Use", not "utilize". "Really important", not "paramount".

**IELTS / TOEFL / exam speaking**: read `ielts-speaking.md`. It has the user's preferred model answer, a Part 2 template, vocabulary level, and exceptions to the usual AI-tell rules (for example, one "not only… but also" is fine there).

**Things that sound memorized and robotic when spoken**
- "Firstly… Secondly… In conclusion…"
- Long lists of three adjectives
- Idioms crammed in to sound fluent ("at the end of the day, it's a piece of cake to think outside the box")
- Perfectly balanced "on the one hand / on the other hand" structures

**What to do**
- Ask (or infer) the context: job interview, IELTS/TOEFL speaking part, self-introduction, presentation, small talk. Also ask the learner's level if it's unclear. A script far above their level will sound memorized when they deliver it.
- Write it the way it should be said. Use short paragraphs that map to breaths, and put a natural pause wherever there's a line break.
- Add only a few fillers and discourse markers. One "I mean" or "actually" every few sentences is natural. Every sentence is a caricature.
- Prefer personal, concrete content. Use placeholders for the learner's real details.
- Keep lengths realistic: about 130–150 spoken words per minute. A 1-minute answer is roughly 130 words, not 300.
- **Extra output for practice**: after the script, add a short list of 3–6 useful phrases from it (chunks the learner can reuse), and optionally mark words that commonly trip up learners on stress or pronunciation. Keep this brief.

**Before / after** (interview: "Tell me about a challenge you faced.")
> One significant challenge I encountered was managing a cross-functional project with tight deadlines. Through effective communication and meticulous planning, I was able to navigate these complexities and deliver the project successfully.

> Sure. So, last year I was put in charge of a launch that had, honestly, way too little time. Three weeks, and two teams that had never worked together. The first week was rough. What actually fixed it was pretty simple. I started a 10-minute call every morning, just to see who was stuck. And we shipped. Two days late, but we shipped.

(The second version still needs the learner's real details. Mark it with placeholders if you don't have them.)

## 4. Short genres

- **Business email**: Lead with the ask. Keep it short. Skip "I hope this email finds you well."
- **LinkedIn**: Avoid the one-sentence-per-line "broetry" format and an "Agree?" ending, which is now its own tell. Write a normal paragraph or two with a real story.
- **Blog**: First person, opinions, and specifics. Use subheadings only if the post is long.
- **Product and marketing copy**: Concrete benefits and numbers. No "seamless", "robust", "elevate", or "unlock".
