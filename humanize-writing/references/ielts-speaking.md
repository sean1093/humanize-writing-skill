## 1. The model answer

> **Cue card:** Describe a public figure you admire. You should say: who this person is · how you learned about them · what they have achieved · and explain why you admire them

```
I'd like to talk about Kobe Bryant, who was a famous NBA basketball player.

I first heard about him by watching NBA games with my classmates when I was in junior high school. At first, I was just interested in his basketball skills, but after watching more of his games, I became a big fan of him.

I admire him not only because he was an amazing basketball player, but also because of his attitude. He was famous for the "Mamba Mentality". It means never giving up and always trying to improve yourself.

I remember that he once had a serious Achilles injury during a game. It was a really serious injury for a basketball player. But he didn't give up. He spent months on rehabilitation and eventually came back to play.

I really admire his resilience. I can understand what he went through better now because I recently had a serious ankle injury myself. The recovery has been painful and frustrating sometimes. But when I think about Kobe and what he went through, it gives me motivation to keep going.

So, for me, Kobe was not just a great basketball player. He was also someone I could learn from. That's why I really admire him.
```

**This is the target style.** Match its length, vocabulary level, structure, and tone. Don't write something more impressive. Write something that sounds like this.

## 2. Why it works

- **Easy to say out loud.** Average sentence is ~12 words, a third of the sentences are 8 words or fewer, and there are no long clauses to trip over.
- **Simple core vocabulary** (famous, interested, amazing, serious, motivation), with a **few** topic words that show range: *rehabilitation, resilience, frustrating, Achilles injury*. That's four or five, not twenty.
- **One concept, explained simply**: "He was famous for the 'Mamba Mentality'. It means never giving up…" The speaker names something specific, then explains it in plain words. This shows vocabulary and fluency at the same time.
- **One concrete story** (the Achilles injury) instead of a list of achievements.
- **A real personal connection** (the speaker's own ankle injury). This is what makes it sound like *this person*, and it's easy to talk about fluently because it actually happened.
- **Natural spoken connectors**: *At first… but after…*, *I remember that…*, *But…*, *So, for me,…*, *That's why…*
- **Short punchy sentences at key moments**: "But he didn't give up." "I really admire his resilience."
- **Covers every cue-card point**, roughly in order, without announcing them ("Now I'll talk about his achievements").

## 3. Part 2 template

Use this as a **skeleton**. Vary the exact wording for each new topic so it doesn't sound memorized. Examiners recognize recycled template phrases.

| # | Part | Length | Pattern (vary the wording) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Intro**: who/what | 1 sentence | "I'd like to talk about [X], who/which [one-line description]." |
| 2 | **How you know it**: when, where, with whom | 2–3 sentences | "I first [heard about / saw / tried] it [when/where/with whom]. At first, I [simple reaction], but after [more experience], I [stronger feeling]." |
| 3 | **Main point**: achievement or quality | 2–3 sentences | State the main reason. Name **one** specific idea, then explain it simply: "It means…" |
| 4 | **One specific story** | 3–4 sentences | "I remember that [one event]." Add a detail. Then a short, punchy sentence. Then what happened. |
| 5 | **Personal connection** | 3–4 sentences | Link it to your own life: "I can understand… because I [real experience]." How it felt, and how this person/thing helps. |
| 6 | **Wrap-up**: answers "explain why" | 2 sentences | "So, for me, [X] was not just [obvious thing]. [It/He/She] was also [deeper thing]. That's why I really [admire/love/remember]…" |

**Length**: 180–250 words, which is about 1.5–2 minutes at a learner's speaking pace. Part 2 allows up to 2 minutes, so don't go over ~260.

## 4. Vocabulary level

- **Core**: simple, high-frequency words a B1–B2 learner uses comfortably: really, very, big, famous, amazing, interested, hard, happy, give up, keep going.
- **Topic words**: 3–6 per answer. Choose words that are **specific to the topic** (rehabilitation, Achilles injury, championship, recipe, volunteer), plus 1–2 **feeling or quality words** (resilience, frustrating, motivation, determined). These are what earn Lexical Resource points. Topic-specific nouns sound natural; random "advanced" adjectives sound memorized.
- **Avoid**: idioms crammed in to impress ("a piece of cake", "once in a blue moon"), formal written words (furthermore, significant, utilize, plethora), and the general AI-tell list.
- If the user's own level is different (they say "band 7–8", or their sample is more advanced), adjust. The model answer is the default.

## 5. Genre exceptions to the usual AI-tell rules

In IELTS speaking, a few things the checker normally flags are fine or even expected:

- **"not only… but also…"** and **"not just X… also Y"**: examiners reward a range of structures. Use **at most one of each per answer**, as the model does. The checker will flag `negation_reframe`. That's expected here.
- **A short wrap-up sentence**: Part 2 asks you to "explain why", so ending with "That's why I really admire him" answers the question. Keep it to 1–2 sentences, and never recap every point.
- **"I'd like to talk about…"**: a normal, natural opener. Fine to use, but vary it now and then ("I'm going to talk about…", "The person I want to talk about is…").
- **Lots of "I" sentence openers**: normal for a first-person story. Don't rewrite them away.

What still counts as a tell, even in IELTS: "Firstly/Secondly/In conclusion", lists of three adjectives, delve/crucial/pivotal-type vocabulary, long multi-clause sentences, and abstract statements with no story.

## 6. Personal stories: real vs example

Real experiences are easier to say fluently and sound more natural, so ask for them (or use the ones the user gives you). If the user hasn't given any:

- Write the answer with a **clearly marked suggested story** that the user can swap out: wrap it in `[…]` or mark it `(example, swap for your own)`. This is practice material, not a factual claim to a third party, so a plausible example story is fine as long as it's labeled.
- Keep facts about **real public figures** accurate. Don't invent achievements, quotes, or events for a real person. If you're unsure of a detail, use something widely known or leave it general.
- In your note, tell the user once that a story from their own life will be easier to remember and deliver.

## 7. Output format for IELTS answers

1. **The answer**, in short paragraphs that follow the template (one breath-group per paragraph).
2. **Word count and estimated time** (e.g. "~210 words, about 1.5–2 min").
3. **Placeholders** to swap for real details, if any.
4. **Useful phrases** (4–6): reusable chunks from the answer, grouped as connectors (At first… but after…) and topic words (rehabilitation, resilience).
5. Optional: 2–3 words with tricky pronunciation or stress (e.g. *Achilles* /əˈkɪliːz/, *rehabilitation*).

If the user wrote to you in Chinese, write items 2–5 in Chinese and keep the answer in English.

## 8. Parts 1 and 3 (shorter answers)

- **Part 1** (personal questions): 2–4 sentences, 30–60 words. Give a direct answer, one reason or detail, and optionally a tiny example. "Yeah, I do. I usually… mostly because… Like last weekend, I…"
- **Part 3** (discussion): 4–7 sentences, 70–120 words. Give an opinion, a reason, an example (it can be general: "for example, in Taiwan…"), and optionally the other side in one clause. Use slightly more abstract vocabulary than Part 2, but keep it spoken: "I think…", "It depends, really.", "On the other hand,…" (once).
