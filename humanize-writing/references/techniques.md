# Rewriting Techniques, with Examples

Each example shows a typical model draft followed by a human-sounding rewrite, and then explains what changed. The point is the reasoning, not the exact wording, so don't copy these phrasings into new pieces.

---

## 1. Kill the throat-clearing opener

**Before**
> In today's fast-paced digital landscape, remote work has become more than just a trend — it's a fundamental shift in how we approach our professional lives.

**After**
> I've worked from my kitchen table for three years now, and I still haven't figured out lunch.

The rewrite drops the scene-setting, the "more than just X, it's Y" frame, and the em dash. It opens on a specific, slightly funny detail that signals a real person is talking. If the user hasn't given you a personal detail like this, use a placeholder or open on a concrete claim instead of inventing one.

---

## 2. Break the uniform rhythm

**Before**
> Effective communication is essential for team success. It helps build trust among team members. It also reduces misunderstandings and conflicts. Ultimately, it leads to better outcomes for everyone involved.

(Sentence lengths: 7, 7, 6, 10. Every sentence is a flat declarative with the same shape.)

**After**
> Most team problems I've seen weren't skill problems. They were somebody assuming somebody else had sent the email. Fix that, and a surprising amount of the friction just goes away.

The rewrite has lengths of 8, 11, and 15, each with a different shape. It's built on one concrete mechanism (the unsent email) rather than four abstract benefits, and the "Ultimately" closer is gone.

---

## 3. Undo the triplet

**Before**
> The new dashboard is faster, more intuitive, and more reliable.

**After**
> The new dashboard loads in under a second. That's the whole pitch, honestly. The old one took eight.

Pick the claim that matters most and prove it. If all three really matter, give them unequal weight and different sentence structures.

---

## 4. Remove the negation-reframe

**Before**
> This isn't just a tool. It's a new way of thinking about productivity.

**After**
> We built it because our own task list had 400 items and no way to tell which ones mattered.

The before is a hollow contrast. The after says what the thing actually is and why it exists.

---

## 5. Cut trailing participle commentary

**Before**
> The city invested $2M in bike lanes, highlighting its commitment to sustainable transportation.

**After**
> The city put $2M into bike lanes. Whether anyone rides them in February is another question.

Don't tell the reader what a fact "highlights". Either let the fact stand or add an actual opinion.

---

## 6. Replace generic examples with specific ones

**Before**
> For example, a small business owner might use this strategy to increase customer engagement.

**After**
> A bakery near my office started texting regulars when the sourdough came out of the oven. Their 7 a.m. line doubled.

Specifics are the strongest human signal. If you don't have a real one, ask the user or leave a placeholder: `[real example from your work]`. Don't fabricate a bakery and pass it off as true.

---

## 7. Drop the recap ending

**Before**
> In conclusion, by prioritizing clear communication, investing in the right tools, and fostering a culture of trust, teams can unlock their full potential and thrive in the modern workplace.

**After**
> Start with the email thing. The rest gets easier.

End on the most useful or most interesting thought, not a summary. Short endings work well when the voice supports them.

---

## 8. Commit to an opinion

**Before**
> While some argue that daily standups are valuable, others feel they can be time-consuming. Ultimately, the right approach depends on your team's unique needs.

**After**
> Daily standups are mostly theater. Twice a week, written, async, has worked better on every team I've been on.

False balance reads as AI. A real writer has a take, and can acknowledge the other side in a clause instead of a paragraph.

---

## 9. Match a provided voice

When the user provides a sample, list its fingerprints before writing:

- Average sentence length, and how much it varies
- How it opens paragraphs (with "So", a question, a blunt claim?)
- Contractions: always, sometimes, never
- Humor style: dry, self-deprecating, none
- Punctuation habits: parentheses, semicolons, ellipses, exclamation points
- Pet words or phrases
- Formality: does it say "folks", "y'all", "one might"?

Then write to those fingerprints. Matching the user's own quirks beats any generic "human" style.
